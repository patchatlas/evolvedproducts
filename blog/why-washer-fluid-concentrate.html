Washer fluid concentrate vs. ready-to-use jugs

Walk into any auto parts store and you'll see the same thing: shelves and pallets of one-gallon jugs of blue washer fluid. Almost everything in those jugs is water.

What's actually in a jug

Washer fluid is a small amount of cleaning ingredients mixed into a lot of water. Winter blends also add an antifreeze, usually an alcohol, so the fluid doesn't freeze. Either way, water makes up most of what you're paying to bottle, ship and carry.

The weight problem

A gallon of water weighs about 8.3 pounds. A shop that goes through 1,000 gallons of washer fluid a year is moving more than four tons of mostly water, plus 1,000 plastic jugs, plus the cardboard and pallets they ship on.

How concentrate changes the math

A concentrate ships only the active part. You add the water where you use it, from the tap or a hose. With a mix rate of 1 oz per 55 gallons, a single 32 oz bottle makes up to 1,760 gallons of fluid. That's enough to replace 1,760 one-gallon jugs.

  • Storage: a few small bottles instead of a stack of jugs or pallets.
  • Waste: one empty bottle instead of a bag full of empty jugs.
  • Freight: you aren't paying to ship water.

What to watch for

Concentrate only works if you mix it right. Measure carefully, use clean water, and label any jug or drum you fill. Also check whether a product is rated for freezing temperatures. Many summer and bug-wash formulas aren't, and they need to be swapped out before winter.
